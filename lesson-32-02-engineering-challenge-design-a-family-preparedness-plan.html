📖 Before You Design
The engineering design process
⏱ 8 min
🎯
Section 1
Criteria and Constraints

Every engineering problem starts with criteria and constraints. For a family plan, criteria might be: everyone knows where to shelter, the kit has three days of supplies, and every family member has a way to contact the others.

Constraints are the limits. Your family may have a small budget, little storage space, or no car. A good plan works within those limits.

🤔
Stop and Think
"Name one criterion and one constraint for YOUR family's plan."
🧪
Section 2
Test, Then Improve

A plan on paper is a design. A drill is the test. When you practice, you find the weak spots: the flashlight with dead batteries, the meeting place no one remembered, or the kit that's too heavy to carry.

Engineers compare results to the criteria, then improve the design. Then they test again. That loop is the heart of engineering.

🧪
Stop and Reason
"Why is finding a problem during a drill actually a good thing?"
🔑 Key Concept

The engineering design process helps you build a family preparedness plan: define the problem with criteria and constraints, brainstorm, design the plan, test it with a drill, improve it, and share it.

Mind-Blowing Fact
Many schools practice fire drills every month. Practicing makes people faster and calmer, because their bodies remember what to do. The same is true for a family preparedness drill.

🔬 Design, Test, Improve
Build your plan and run a drill
⏱ 30 min

Follow the steps to design your family's preparedness plan, test it with a short drill, and improve it.

1

Define the problem

Write the disaster your plan is for. List at least three criteria and two constraints in your notebook.

2

Design your plan

Fill in the plan table below in your notebook: shelter spot, evacuation route, meeting place, out-of-town contact, kit list, and special needs.

Part of the planMy designAfter testing: keep or change?

🚗 Evacuation route

📞 Out-of-town contact
🎒 Kit list (at least 6 items)
🐾 Special needs (pets, medicine, babies)
⏱️ Drill time (1st try / 2nd try)
3

Run a drill

With a family member if possible, pretend a warning was just issued. Time how long it takes to gather the kit and reach your shelter spot. Write the time.

4

Check the criteria

Did the plan meet every criterion? Write what worked and what went wrong.

5

Improve and retest

Change at least one part of your plan to fix a problem. If you can, run the drill again and compare the times.

Self-Check
Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you! See how much you understand.
Which of these is a CONSTRAINT for a family preparedness plan?
AEveryone knows the meeting place
BThe family has only $40 to spend on supplies
CThe kit has three days of water
✅ Correct! A constraint is a limit on the design. A small budget limits what you can buy.
❌ Not quite. "Everyone knows the meeting place" and "three days of water" are criteria: things the plan must do. Which choice is a limit?
During a drill, your family takes 8 minutes to find the flashlight. What should an engineer do next?
AImprove the plan, like storing the flashlight in the kit, then test again
BGive up on the plan
CSkip drills from now on
✅ Correct! Engineers use test results to improve the design, then test again to see if it got better.
❌ Not quite. A problem found in a drill is useful evidence. What does an engineer do with evidence from a test?
Why is it important to update a family plan every year?
APlans expire like milk
BFamilies, supplies, and needs change over time
CIt is required by law
✅ Correct! Food and batteries get old, kids grow, families move, and needs change. A plan has to keep up.
❌ Not quite. Think about what changes in a family and its supplies over a year.
