📖 Before You Begin
Safety check and the conductor-test plan
⏱ 7 min
⚖️
Section 1
Safety First

Today you'll work with a battery flashlight only. Small batteries like AA and AAA are safe to handle. Never test anything in a wall outlet, a plug, or a cord — the electricity in your home is far stronger and can hurt you badly.

Ask an adult before you open the flashlight. Don't let foil or a paperclip touch both ends of a battery at the same time — that can make the battery hot. Put everything back together when you finish.

🤔
Stop and Think
"Why is a small battery safe to test with, but a wall outlet is never safe? What is different about them?"
📦
Section 2
The Test at a Glance

A flashlight is a closed circuit in a tube. The battery is the power source. A metal spring and metal strips are the wires. The bulb does the job. The switch opens and closes the loop. You'll make a tiny gap between the battery and the spring, then fill that gap with one material at a time. If the bulb lights, the material let electricity through.

🧪
Stop and Reason
"If you put something in the gap and the bulb stays dark, what does that tell you about the material?"
📊
Section 3
Recording Your Results

For each material, you'll fill out one row of a table. First write your prediction. Then test it and write what the bulb did. Last, decide: conductor or insulator?

Real-World Connection: Electricians test materials too. They choose copper for wires because it conducts well. They wrap those wires in plastic and wear rubber-soled shoes because insulators keep the electricity where it belongs.

🔑 Key Concept

A conductor in the gap keeps the circuit closed, so the bulb lights. An insulator in the gap makes the circuit open, so the bulb stays dark. The bulb is your evidence.

👆

Open it. Predict. Test. The bulb tells you whether each material is a conductor or an insulator.
🤯
Mind-Blowing Fact
Pencil "lead" is not lead at all — it is graphite, a kind of carbon. Graphite can carry electricity, which makes it one of the few non-metal conductors you might find on your desk.
Go Deeper — What if nothing lights at all?

First, take every material out and switch on the flashlight. If it still won't light, the batteries may be dead or in backward. Check that each battery's + end points the way the flashlight shows.

If the flashlight works empty but a metal won't light it, the metal may not be touching both the battery and the spring. Coins and paperclips can have a thin coating or dirt on them. Press the cap a little tighter, or rub the metal with a tissue, and try again.

3
🔬 The Flashlight Gap Test
Six materials. One gap. Light or no light?
⏱ 20 min total

Gather your materials. You'll need: a flashlight that uses AA or AAA batteries, and six small flat test pieces — a square of aluminum foil, a coin, a paperclip (bent flat), a square of paper, a square of plastic bag, and a rubber band (folded flat). Each piece should be big enough to cover the end of a battery.

⚠️

Before you start: Ask an adult before you open the flashlight. Battery flashlights only — never test anything in an outlet or a cord. Don't let foil or a paperclip touch both ends of one battery. Don't shine the flashlight into anyone's eyes.

1

Open the flashlight and trace the loop

First, switch the flashlight on and off to make sure it works. With an adult's OK, unscrew the end cap and slide out the batteries. Look for the metal spring, the metal strips or contacts, the switch, and the bulb. Sketch the loop in your notebook: battery → spring → metal path → bulb → back to the battery.

2

Predict, then test the gap

Put the batteries back in. Write a prediction for each material in your table. Then slide the first material flat between the end of the battery and the spring, so the battery doesn't touch the spring. Screw the cap back on and switch the flashlight on. Did the bulb light? Record it.

3

Test all six materials

Take the first material out and switch on the flashlight with nothing in the gap — it should light. Then test the next material the same way. Keep going until you've tested all six. Put the flashlight back together when you finish.

4

Compare your results

Look back at your results. Which materials made the bulb light? Which kept it dark? Write CONDUCTOR or INSULATOR in the last column for each one.

Self-Check
Quick Self-Check
Not graded — just for you! See how much you understand.
When you opened the flashlight, which part was the power source?
AThe bulb
BThe battery
CThe metal spring
✅ Correct! The battery is the power source — it stores chemical energy that becomes electrical energy. The spring connects the battery to the bulb. The bulb is what the electricity powers.
❌ Not quite. The bulb USES electricity but doesn't make it. The spring CONNECTS, but doesn't store power. What part inside is full of chemical energy waiting to be released?
A paperclip and a rubber band are both small. Which one would best replace a flashlight's spring?
AThe paperclip — it's a metal conductor
BThe rubber band — it's flexible like a spring
CEither one would work the same
✅ Correct! A paperclip is metal — electricity can flow through it. A rubber band is an insulator — electricity can't flow through it. The paperclip would complete the circuit; the rubber band would break it.
❌ Not quite. Flexibility doesn't matter for a circuit. What matters is whether electricity can flow through the material. Rubber is an insulator — it blocks electricity.
Surprise fact: pencil graphite (the dark "lead") can actually conduct electricity. What would happen if you put a thick line of pencil graphite between the battery and the bulb in a circuit?
AThe bulb would light up — graphite acts like a conductor
BNothing would happen — pencils are wood
CThe bulb would explode
✅ Correct! Graphite is a conductor (it's a form of carbon, like coal). Electricity can flow right through a thick pencil line and complete a circuit — though slower than through metal.
❌ Not quite. The pencil "lead" is actually graphite — a type of carbon that conducts electricity. The wood around it is the insulator, but graphite itself works as a wire.
