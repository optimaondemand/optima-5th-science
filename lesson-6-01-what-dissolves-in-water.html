📖 The Lesson
What's actually happening to the sugar?
⏱ 15 min
💧
Section 1
When Something Dissolves

When sugar dissolvesdissolveTo break apart into tiny pieces that spread through a liquid. in water, its particles get pulled apart by the water particles. The sugar breaks down into pieces so small you can't see them anymore — but they're still there. You'd taste them right away. You'd find them with a scale.

Remember from Module 3: matter doesn't disappear. When you dissolve 5 grams of sugar in 100 grams of water, the cup still weighs 105 grams. The sugar particles are just hiding — spread evenly all through the water.

🤔
Stop and Think
"If sugar particles are spread all through the water, why does the water still look clear? Wouldn't you expect to see something?"
🥤
Section 2
Solutions vs. Other Mixtures

When something dissolves completely into a liquid, you get a solutionsolutionA mixture where one thing has fully dissolved into another. — a special kind of mixturemixtureTwo or more materials combined.. Sugar water is a solution. Salt water is a solution. Both look like plain water, but they're not — they have something extra dissolved in them.

A cup of sand and water is also a mixture, but it's NOT a solution. The sand is still visible at the bottom. The parts haven't blended into one. That's the difference: in a solution, you can't see the dissolved material anymore.

🧪
Stop and Reason
"If you let your sugar water sit on the counter for a week with the cup covered, would the sugar settle to the bottom like sand? Why or why not?"
✅
Section 3
Soluble or Insoluble?

Materials are either solublesolubleAble to dissolve in a liquid. in water (they dissolve) or insolubleinsolubleNot able to dissolve in a liquid. (they don't). Sugar, salt, baking soda, and instant coffee are soluble. Sand, oil, flour, and pepper are insoluble.

Real-World Connection: Whenever you mix lemonade powder into water, season pasta water with salt, or stir hot cocoa, you're making a solution. The taste, color, or smell tells you the powder is still there — even though you can't see it.

🔑 Key Concept

A soluble material breaks apart into tiny particles that spread evenly through water, making a solution. An insoluble material keeps its grains together — they just sink or float instead of dissolving.

⚖️ Where Does the Sugar Go? Conservation of Matter in Solutions

When sugar dissolves in water, it seems to disappear. But here's the key question: did the matter actually vanish? No! If you weigh the water before adding sugar, then weigh the solution after the sugar dissolves, the total mass is exactly the same. The sugar particles are still there — they're just too small to see, spread evenly through the water.

This is conservation of matter: matter is never created or destroyed, even in a solution. You can prove the sugar is still there by tasting the water (sweet!) or by letting the water evaporate — the sugar crystals reappear. Scientists compare the properties of a solution (like taste, density, or boiling point) to the properties of the original materials to confirm that both are still present.

Mind-Blowing Fact
A single cup of room-temperature water can dissolve about 200 grams of sugar — almost half a cup! But it can only dissolve about 80 grams of salt. Every material has its own limit.
Go Deeper — Why does sugar dissolve but sand doesn't?

It comes down to how the particles are held together. Sugar particles are loosely bound to each other and water particles are good at pulling them apart. Once a sugar particle is alone in the water, it just floats around with all the others.

Sand is mostly tiny grains of a mineral called quartz. The particles inside each grain are locked together very tightly — much more tightly than water can pull. So each grain stays whole, and gravity does the rest.

Sort each material

2

Watch out for tricky ones

Cocoa powder makes the water turn brown — does that mean it dissolved? Look for "clumps at bottom." Cloudy water means tiny pieces are floating, NOT dissolved. The water has to look clear for the material to be truly soluble.
