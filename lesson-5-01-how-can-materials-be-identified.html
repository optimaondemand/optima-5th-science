📖 The Lesson
Five ways to figure out what something is made of
⏱ 15 min
🧱
Section 1
Matter vs. Material

Everything around you is matter. But not all matter is made of the same stuff. A penny, a paper clip, and a juice box are all matter — but the penny is mostly copper, the paper clip is steel, and the juice box is mostly cardboard. The materialmaterialThe stuff something is made of. is the kind of stuff something is made out of.

Scientists figure out what something is made of by looking at its propertiespropertyA feature you can observe or measure about a material. — measurable features that don't depend on opinion. Two scientists checking the same property of the same object should always get the same answer.

🤔
Stop and Think
"'Pretty' isn't a property a scientist would use. 'Red,' 'hard,' and '4 grams' are. What's the difference?"
🧲
Section 2
Five Tests That Work on Almost Anything

You don't need a real lab to test properties. Five simple tests cover most everyday materials: magnetismmagnetismWhether a magnet sticks to a material. (does a magnet stick?), hardnesshardnessHow hard it is to scratch a material. (can you scratch it?), texturetextureHow something feels. (how does it feel?), color, and mass (how heavy?).

Each test gives you one piece of evidenceevidenceObservations that support a claim.. Put a few pieces together and you can usually figure out the material. A paper clip is magnetic, shiny, light, and bendy — that points to steel. A penny is shiny, heavy for its size, but NOT magnetic — that points to copper.

🧪
Stop and Reason
"Why is one test usually not enough? What might happen if you only checked color and decided based on that?"
⚠️
Section 3
Watch Out for Lookalikes

Some materials trick you on a single test. Aluminum foil and silver wrapping paper both look shiny and silver — but only one is metal. A wooden block painted gray looks like stone, but the scratch test gives it away. That's why scientists test multiple properties before they call something identified.

Real-World Connection: Recycling centers use property tests every single day. Magnets pull out the steel cans. Air jets push the lighter plastics one way and the heavier glass another. Different properties → different sorting bins.

🔑 Key Concept

You can identify what something is made of by testing its observable and measurable properties. The more properties you test, the stronger your identification.

Five tests. A lot of evidence. Most everyday materials give themselves away.

Mind-Blowing Fact
Aluminum looks shiny like steel but doesn't stick to a magnet. That's how recycling machines can pull out steel cans and leave the aluminum cans behind — sorting thousands of cans per hour with just one big magnet.
Go Deeper — Properties scientists use that you don't see every day

Beyond the five you used today, scientists test even more properties when they need to be sure: density (mass divided by volume), boiling point (temperature where a liquid turns to gas), conductivity (does electricity flow through it?), and even how a material reflects different colors of light.

Modern recycling factories combine three or four of these tests in giant machines. The machines never need a label — they just measure.

Object B — tested by Devin

Magnetism: No, magnet doesn't stick at all. Hardness: Medium — my fingernail can leave a small mark. Color: Dull, brownish, with darker grain lines. Mass: Light for its size. Texture: Rough, slightly bumpy.

Object C — tested by Avery

Magnetism: No, doesn't stick. Hardness: Soft — fingernail leaves a dent. Color: Bright red, smooth and shiny. Mass: Very light, almost feels like nothing. Texture: Smooth and slightly slippery.
